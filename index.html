    Guide

    Everything you see is computed for the instant on the clock: where each body is, which way it faces, where its shadow falls and which stars lie behind it.

    Getting around

    • Look around. Drag to turn the view; scroll or pinch to zoom; right-drag or drag with two fingers to move it sideways.
    • Visit a body. Click it, its label or its name in the Bodies list, and the camera glides over. Choose it again to fly in close, and a third time to fly back out. If you have moved the view sideways, choosing it again first brings it back to the centre. The arrow beside a planet lists its moons; choosing the Bodies tab again folds the list away. On phones and upright tablets the tabs show only their icons: a ringed planet for Bodies, a gear for Settings and a question mark for the Guide.
    • Run time. The round button plays and pauses time. The speed slider beside it runs from real time on the left to years per second on the right, and the clock with the circular arrow runs time backward.
    • Jump in time. The calendar button goes to any date from 1000 to 2999, and Now comes back to the present.
    • Eclipses and transits, the button between the calendar and Now, lists past and coming events. Choose one to watch it.
    • Settings, the tab beside Bodies, turn orbits, labels, moons, the spin axis, the stars, the Milky Way, the Sun’s glare and Earth’s city lights on and off, and set Lock. Below them, two sliders have reality at their left end: Scale runs from the real scale to an overview, and Night sides from real, dark night sides to lit ones. All are kept for your next visit.
    • Hide the interface with the eye beside the clock’s time zone to see only the sky; click or tap empty space to bring it back. Turning, zooming and choosing bodies keep working meanwhile.

    More, for the curious:

    More about the controls
    • The camera glides to a body without changing your zoom or viewing angle (it only backs off if it would end up inside the body). Flying in close turns it toward the sunlit side if you were looking at the night side.
    • Lock (in the settings) keeps the camera travelling with the selected body. Unlock it to let the body drift away, and lock again to catch up with it. Right-drag, shift-drag, the arrow keys or a two-finger drag move the view sideways; locked, the camera keeps following the body from there.
    • The speed slider runs from real time (1×) at its left end to about five years per second at its right. Up to 60× the speed is shown as a multiple of real time, faster as simulated time per second (“5 min/s”, “2 d/s”, “1 yr/s”). Play and Pause keep the chosen speed: paused, it is shown dimmed. Running backward keeps the speed too, and marks it with a −.
    • The calendar takes dates in the time the clock shows (local or UTC). On a phone it opens the phone’s own date picker; on a computer type or pick a date and press Set (or Enter).
    • The button above the clock switches between Local time, UTC and Scientific, which shows Universal Time (UT, Earth’s rotation, UT1) and adds TT (the uniform time the planets move by), ΔT = TT − UT and the Julian Date. The model takes UTC to be UT1; the two never differ by more than 0.9 s.
    • Night sides (in the settings) adds a light from where you are looking, so night sides and the dark of an eclipse show their surface; the further right, the brighter. It is not real light: the shadows themselves stay exactly where they are. At Real, night sides are black as a camera exposed for daylight sees them; your eye, adapted to the dark, would see the Moon’s dark side faintly lit by Earth (earthshine). Sun glare turns off the glare around the Sun, which helps to see what lies close to it, such as a planet crossing the Sun’s disc.
    • The information panel starts folded down to the body’s name: tap it (or +) to open it, and – folds it again. On a phone or an upright tablet, tap an open panel to show all of its data.
    Overview and real scale

    At real scale the Solar System is almost entirely empty: if the Sun were a grapefruit, Earth would be a grain of sand 13 m away and Neptune 390 m away. This is how the model first opens. The overview shrinks distances and enlarges bodies so everything fits on one screen, and the Scale slider in the settings morphs smoothly between the two, from real scale on the left to the overview on the right. Directions are never distorted, so alignments such as eclipses and conjunctions look right at every setting. Bodies too small to see are marked by their labels; hovering over one circles it.

    Shadows and eclipses

    Shadows are computed for every point on a surface from the true sizes and distances of the Sun and of the body in the way. They are therefore correct at every scale setting, even when the Moon is drawn closer than it really is. The Sun’s limb darkening is included (the light left in a deep partial shadow comes from the Sun’s redder edge, which tints it brown), and so is the light’s travel time: the sunlight hitting Earth passed the Moon 1.3 s earlier, which moves a solar eclipse 35 km along its track.

    During a lunar eclipse the Moon turns copper-red, because the only light reaching it has been bent and reddened by Earth’s atmosphere. Saturn’s rings cast shadows on the planet and Saturn casts one on its rings. Jupiter’s moons cast theirs on Jupiter and disappear into its shadow, whose flattened shape is taken into account (it lengthens Io’s eclipses by three minutes). Pluto and Charon eclipse each other only in two seasons of each 248-year orbit, the last in 1985–1990.

    One liberty: where a moon’s shadow falls on its planet, the partial shadow is drawn darker than a light meter would record. On a screen a surface at half light looks almost fully lit, and the Moon’s partial shadow, thousands of km across, would be only a faint smudge; it is deepened a little, and the full shadow, where the eclipse is total, stays the one black core. Where every shadow begins and ends, and the full shadow itself, are exact. Earth’s shadow on the Moon needs no such help and is drawn as measured.

    How accurate is it?

    The positions were compared with NASA JPL’s DE440 ephemeris (via Horizons) over 1800–2050:

    WhatLargest error, 1800–2050
    Planets as seen from Earth< 25″ (the eye resolves ~60″)
    Moon< 21 km (12″)
    Io, Europa, Ganymede, Callisto< 900 km (≤ 0.12° of their orbits)
    Titan · Phobos · Deimos< 850 km · 20 km · 56 km
    Mimas, Enceladus, Tethys, Dione, Rhea< 270 km (< 0.1° of their orbits)
    Iapetus< 1,400 km (0.02°)
    Triton · Charon< 100 km · 1 km
    Solar eclipses (1919, 1999, 2024, 2027)greatest eclipse within 5 km and 5 s of NASA’s canon

    Outside 1800–2050 an Extrapolated tag appears next to the clock. The planets stay good to about an arcminute for centuries; the moons of Mars, Saturn, Neptune and Pluto are extrapolated fits.

    Earth’s rotation depends on ΔT, the slowly growing lag of Earth’s rotation behind atomic time. The model uses the measured values (telescopic timings since 1657, atomic clocks since 1955) and the official prediction to 2033. Before 1657 ΔT is estimated from historical eclipse records and uncertain by minutes; beyond the 2030s it cannot be predicted well, so eclipse tracks can shift east–west by tens of km.

    Dates before 15 October 1582 are in the Julian calendar, as in historical records and NASA’s eclipse canon. The Gregorian reform then skipped ten days.

    What the picture shows

    The Sun is the only light, so night sides are black, as a camera exposed for daylight sees them (the Night sides slider in the settings lights them); Earth’s night side shows its city lights. Distant worlds are brightened so they stay visible (Neptune receives 1/900 of the sunlight Earth does).

    The Sun’s disc is drawn as it looks in white light through a filter: featureless, darker and redder toward its edge. Around it is the glare it would cause in an eye or a camera exposed for the planets, which drowns the nearby stars; it fades as the disc grows large, as it does in a close or telescope view. Its fine radial streaks are light scattered inside the eye’s lens, and like the real ones they change as you look around. Behind the stars lies the faint glow of the Milky Way, with the Magellanic Clouds and the Andromeda galaxy.

    Earth’s atmosphere is a thin blue haze toward the limb, lit only where sunlight reaches it. The Moon and other dusty airless worlds scatter light back toward the Sun, so a full Moon is evenly bright to its edge instead of shading like a ball. Every moon turns according to the IAU rotation models, including the slight rocking (libration) that keeps the Moon from showing exactly the same face.

    Each orbit line is the ellipse the body would follow if nothing but its primary pulled on it, fitted to its motion at this instant. Earth’s is the ellipse of the Earth–Moon pair’s centre of mass (4,700 km from Earth’s centre, inside the Earth), drawn through Earth’s centre: Earth itself weaves a few thousand km to either side of it every month. From Saturn outward the ellipses are drawn around the Solar System’s centre of mass, which the Sun itself circles because of Jupiter’s pull.

    Some things are representative rather than exact:

    • Cloud patterns on Earth, Venus, Jupiter and the other giants are snapshots (Venus’s clouds do circle the planet every four days, as the real ones do), and sunspots are not shown.
    • The fine structure of Saturn’s rings comes from an image whose opacity is scaled to measured values.
    • Phobos and Deimos are drawn as ellipsoids of their true dimensions.
    • Parts of Charon and Triton have never been seen, because they were in polar night when New Horizons and Voyager 2 flew past; they are filled in plain grey. Voyager saw Triton’s colours only through orange, violet and ultraviolet filters, so its map is shown in grey, tinted with the moon’s overall colour.
